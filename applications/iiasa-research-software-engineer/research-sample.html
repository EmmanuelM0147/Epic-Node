A Baseline Classifier for Inflammatory Speech in Nigerian Social Media

Research sample · Emmanuel Okeowo · 2026

Code, notebook, and trained artefacts: github.com/EmmanuelM0147/inflammatory_speech_flagger · Live demo: inflammatoryspeechflagger.vercel.app

Summary

I built a reproducible, interpretable baseline that flags potentially inflammatory posts in a Nigerian context, evaluated it on a held-out set, wrote up where it fails, and deployed it as a monitored service. It is a small independent project, not peer-reviewed work. I am sending it because it shows how I handle a dataset, a model, and its validation end to end.

Problem

Generic moderation models are mostly trained on Western data and miss hate speech patterns tied to local tribal, religious, and political tensions, such as the herder conflicts, Biafra separatism, and inter-ethnic rivalry. The goal was a transparent first baseline that a platform could audit before investing in heavier models.

Data

  • Nigerian Based Hate Dataset (Mendeley Data, DOI 10.17632/r5jsynhxsx.1): 20,176 tweets with binary labels, 4,801 hate (23.8%) and 15,375 non-hate.
  • Considered and rejected: NaijaHate (worldbank/NaijaHate) has richer three-class and target-group labels, but it distributes tweet IDs only. Getting the text would have required paid X API access, so I chose the dataset that ships its full text and recorded the trade-off.

Method

  • Preprocessing: lowercase; strip URLs, mentions, and punctuation; collapse whitespace.
  • Split: stratified 80/20 train/test (random_state=42), so the class ratio is preserved and runs are repeatable.
  • Features: TF-IDF on unigrams and bigrams, top 5,000 features, min_df=2.
  • Model: logistic regression with class_weight='balanced' to offset the 24/76 imbalance.
  • Output: each post returns a flag, a confidence score, and the TF-IDF terms that most influenced the decision.

Overall accuracy is 0.84, but I treat hate-class recall and F1 as the primary metrics because of the class imbalance. The model catches three in four hateful posts at the cost of 418 false flags, which suits a review queue rather than automatic removal.

The non-hate figures and both F1 values are computed directly from the confusion matrix in the notebook.

Validation in use

The model is served through a FastAPI service. Every prediction is logged with its confidence, threshold, and latency. Users can mark a result right or wrong, and a monitoring page shows live metrics, recent errors, user feedback, and a confidence histogram. That gives a path to catch drift and collect correction labels after deployment, not only at training time.

Limitations

  • Vocabulary dependence: the dataset was built around known hate terms. In my testing the model handles direct, keyword-heavy posts well and misses calm, essay-style posts that carry the same prejudice.
  • Binary labels: there is no offensive-but-not-hateful tier.
  • Linguistic coverage: Pidgin, Yoruba, Hausa, Igbo, and code-switching are under-represented in about 20,000 tweets.
  • Explanations are heuristic: the top TF-IDF terms are useful for review but are not SHAP or LIME attributions.

Next steps

Fine-tune a transformer such as DistilBERT to capture meaning beyond vocabulary, add formally worded training examples, move to three-class labels, and replace the term-based explanation with SHAP or LIME.

Relevance to GAINS

The habits carry over directly: document why a data source was chosen, fix the split and seed so results can be reproduced, report the metric that matches the question rather than the flattering one, write down the limitations, and keep checking outputs after release.
